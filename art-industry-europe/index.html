The ground

The market you would be entering

A $59.6 billion world market — about ¥9.4 trillion. Two of its four largest national markets are in Europe.

The United Kingdom is the second-largest art market on earth and France is the fourth. Between them they turn over $15.0bn (¥2.36tn) a year — before Switzerland, Germany, Italy or Spain are counted at all.

Everything in this deck is stated in US dollars, with the conversion date printed, and a yen gloss where the number affects your budget.

The part we will not hide

This market is smaller than it was a decade ago

In 2015 the world art market was $63.8bn. In 2025 it was $59.6bn — and that is before inflation.

The United Kingdom's share has gone from 21% in 2016 to 18% in 2025. You will open the same report and see the same arrows, so we are putting them up first.

We are not going to argue that this line is about to turn around. There is no European growth story anywhere in this deck. The strength we are claiming is structural, not financial — and it is on the next slide.

The turn

What a sales figure cannot measure

Market share measures where a transaction clears.
It does not measure where the value was conferred.

What the last chart counted

Turnover, attributed to the country of the seller. A painting sold from a New York booth is a New York sale — wherever it was made, shown, or written about.

What it cannot count

The biennial that put the artist in front of forty curators at once. The catalogue essay a specialist will still be citing in ten years. The museum acquisition that puts a floor under every price that follows.

Why that is your business

You are not choosing a market to sell in. You are choosing a market to be validated in — and then selling wherever you like, Tokyo included.

Turnover follows capital. Reputation follows institutions.
For a decade the two have been moving apart.

The asset

Institutional density with no equivalent anywhere

Six cities, each with its own legitimating machinery, all within a short flight of one another. No other region concentrates this many independent validators in this little space.

London

Tate Modern · Frieze London · Serpentine

The English-language critical press. What is written here is read everywhere, including by your own collectors.

Paris

Centre Pompidou · Art Basel Paris, Grand Palais · Bourse de Commerce · Fondation Louis Vuitton

Private museums that both exhibit and acquire — the rarest combination in Europe.

Basel

Art Basel · Fondation Beyeler · Kunstmuseum Basel

The week the trade uses to calibrate itself. A placement here is read as a verdict by everyone who was not there.

Berlin

Hamburger Bahnhof · the Kunstverein and residency circuit

Little market, dense curatorial layer. Reputations are drafted here and priced elsewhere.

Milan

Fondazione Prada · Pirelli HangarBicocca

A private collecting base with real depth, and Venice two hours away.

Madrid

Museo Reina Sofía · ARCOmadrid

The entrance to a Spanish-language network that reaches well beyond Europe. We return to this at the end.

And the certifying events that belong to no single city: Venice Biennale (since 1895) · documenta, Kassel (every five years) · Manifesta (nomadic). These are the invitations that change a CV permanently.

Sufficiency, not growth

Big enough to be worth the freight

The question is not “is Europe growing?” It is “is Europe big enough?”

Those are different questions and only the second one is yours. The UK and France together turn over $15.0bn a year — 1.8 times the entire Chinese market, and that is before Switzerland, Germany, Italy and Spain.

A market can be flat for a decade and still absorb everything you can ship into it. Sufficiency is the test. Growth is somebody else's argument.

Función: suficiencia. Eje desde cero, sin línea de tendencia, ninguna afirmación de crecimiento (criterio 1).

The one line that rises

Paris, after Brexit

France grew 9% in 2025 while the UK grew 2%. At 8% of the world market, France is at its highest standing in over a decade.

The fair week moved with it. FIAC lost the Grand Palais in 2022 to what became Paris+ par Art Basel; in 2024 it was renamed Art Basel Paris and moved into the restored Grand Palais itself. Bourse de Commerce and Fondation Louis Vuitton now programme at a scale that pulls curators through the city every October.

Read this correctly: Paris is gaining within Europe. This is a reallocation, not European growth — the UK line in the same frame is flat.

The money here is durable

Art is held as wealth, not as decoration

Ultra-high-net-worth individuals held an estimated $2.56 trillion in art and collectibles in 2024, projected to reach $3.47 trillion by 2030.

An estimated $992bn of art and collectibles is expected to change hands over the next decade as wealth passes between generations. Money that is advised, insured, valued and inherited does not evacuate the category in a bad year.

Deloitte is used here for collector wealth and confidence only. It does not cover artist populations or stylistic trends, and this deck does not ask it to.

Demand already demonstrated

Japanese painting already sits at the top of this market

In 2023 the best-selling contemporary artist at auction in the world was Yayoi Kusama. The third was Yoshitomo Nara.

The question “will buyers pay serious money for a Japanese painter?” has an answer, and it is yes. Nara's Knife Behind Back made HK$195.7m at Sotheby's Hong Kong in October 2019.

But look at where it clears: 80% of Kusama's auction lots sold in Hong Kong. The trade settles in Asia. The retrospectives that built the name ran through Madrid, Paris and London in 2011–12. That gap is this entire deck.

Where the opportunity lives

The gap

There is a class of painter who is established in Japan and absent from Europe. Not rejected by Europe. Absent from it.

What they already have

A domestic market that clears. Decades of production behind them. Collectors who know the name. And a dealer holding real depth of inventory — which is the part that matters here.

What they do not have

A European exhibition history. A bibliography in a European language. A curator in Paris or Berlin who can say the name without looking it up.

What the absence costs

An artist established at home and unknown abroad is priced by one market only. That ceiling is not made of quality. It is made of absence — and absence is the one constraint a dealer can actually remove.

Honesty about this slide: we are naming the gap, not sizing it. No reliable count of working artists by nationality exists — no registry keeps one. Anything that claims to size this is a proxy (gallery rosters, fair and biennial participation, cultural-employment statistics), and we would label it as such.

The mechanism

How a European show pays you back

  1. 1

    A European institutional or biennial exhibition

  2. 2

    Critical coverage and permanent CV weight

  3. 3

    A higher primary price, defensible to the next buyer

  4. 4

    A deeper, more liquid secondary market

  5. 5

    A larger margin on the inventory you already hold in Tokyo

This is not only about selling new work in Europe.
It is about revaluing the stock already sitting in your warehouse.

A dealer accumulates inventory in established artists, not emerging ones. So the opportunity here is not launching careers. It is re‑validating, in Europe, careers that were already made in Japan.

Timing

Why now: what the yen actually does

The yen does not make Europe cheaper. It makes European proceeds bigger.

A $100,000 sale converted at 2020's rate was ¥10.7m. At today's rate it is ¥15.8m — 47% more yen for the same dollar price. Your costs inflate by the same 47%, but costs are one-off and bounded, and a higher price level applies to everything you own.

This argument is not load-bearing, and we will not pretend it is. Currency reverses. If the yen strengthens tomorrow, the institutional case, the size of the market and the rise of Paris are exactly where they were — which is why they came first and this comes fourth.

Función: urgencia. Divergencia consciente respecto a la spec §3, que hablaba de "momento barato para entrar": un yen débil encarece los costes europeos en yenes, no los abarata. The ask

A staged entry you can budget

Not a strategy. One cycle, two tracks, twenty‑four months, and a date on which you are allowed to stop.

Track A One fair, as a test

  • One European fair. One booth. Three or four painters, no more.
  • Budget lines: stand (quoted by the fair) · freight · insurance · travel · import VAT at 5–5.5%.
  • Measured by: works placed · new collector names captured · press pickups · curator conversations opened.

The fair pays part of its own bill and buys the relationships.

Track B One institutional route, in parallel

  • A curator‑led group show, a Kunstverein, or a museum conversation begun and kept warm.
  • Budget lines: time · shipping · a proper catalogue. No sales expectation.
  • Measured by: one confirmed institutional showing scheduled within twenty‑four months.

This track is the one that actually produces the revaluation.

The gate, agreed now rather than later: if Track A recovers 60% of its cost and Track B has one date confirmed, you fund a second cycle. If neither, you stop — and the loss is a number you knew before you started. Doing only Track A is a sales trip. Doing only Track B is philanthropy.

Función: decisión presupuestable, no apelación cultural (criterio 7). El 60% es una propuesta de política, no un dato — se negocia en la sala.

Optionality — after the ask, on purpose

Spain is a door to Latin America

This is not part of the decision on the previous slide. It is worth knowing that it exists.

ARCOmadrid looks west

Madrid's fair has a structural Latin American orientation that no other European fair has: the galleries, the curators and the invited guests come from both sides of the Atlantic.

One language, many markets

Spanish carries a collector base across Mexico, Colombia, Argentina and Chile. A press notice written in Madrid is legible in all of them without translation.

Low marginal cost

If a European cycle is already running, adding Madrid is one more stop on a route you are already paying for — not a second geography to open from zero.

Spain grew 6% in 2025. And note which museum keeps appearing in the evidence on slide 12: the Reina Sofía, in Madrid, twice. Say yes to Europe first — this one keeps.
